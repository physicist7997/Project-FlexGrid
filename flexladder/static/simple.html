2One system, two jobs

The same homes, app and planner do both. The everyday job pays for the system and keeps it tested, so it already works on the worst night.

A September heat storm, like Sept 8, 2024, when an overloaded LADWP distributing station forced 30–60 minute rotating outages. At 6:00 PM a Valley substation is overloading. The utility calls: cut MW within 30 minutes and hold it for , or they start switching whole neighborhoods off.
A hot weekday in July. Yesterday afternoon the utility announced a peak event: trim MW from 5:00 PM for , when power is most expensive. Homes that help earn bill credits.

3What FlexGrid did, minute by minute

The planner picks the least disruptive devices first; this list is in the order they respond (batteries in about a minute, thermostats in about five). It only uses devices whose owners signed up, that are reporting and running right now, and only within the owner's limits.

    5Over the next hours, honestly

    FlexGrid re-plans every 15 minutes. Thermostat savings fade as homes warm up, and water heaters and pool pumps can only wait so long, so it reports what it can really deliver.

    Throw a curveball

    Something breaks mid-event. FlexGrid re-plans instantly with what's still working.

    6The deal: who pays, who earns

    One yearly contract pays for both jobs. It's priced on of verified flexibility: the least FlexGrid held at any point in this event, so the utility only pays for what it can count on.
